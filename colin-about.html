I grew up in the East Bay.

The kind of place where you can be at a Giants game on a Tuesday and in the Sierras by Thursday. I bled orange and black, lost my voice at Warriors playoff runs, and stayed loyal to the St. Mary's Gaels through some genuinely rough years.

Geography shapes you. The Bay gave me access to both city and wilderness from a young age, and I've been chasing that combination ever since. I carry the Bay with me everywhere — the way it feels normal to want to be outside, the assumption that good things are worth a drive.

Engineering wasn't a career choice.

I started taking things apart when I was four. TV remotes, mainly. Not to break them — I needed to see inside. That compulsion never really went away. By the time I had to pick a path, engineering was just the closest word for what I already was.

Human-Centered Engineering at Boston College caught my eye specifically because it took that compulsion seriously. Rigorous technical degree, yes — but grounded in BC's Jesuit mission of building what the world actually needs. Engineering as a tool for people, not just systems.

The trail taught me things classrooms couldn't.

I grew up hiking with my family and spent summers in the Sierras. There's something about carrying everything you need on your back — weight, weather, route, risk — that strips a problem down to its essentials.

At BC I found the Outdoor Adventure Club and trained to become a trip leader. Planning and guiding multi-day backpacking trips for students who've never slept outside before is a different kind of engineering problem: every variable matters, and the stakes are real. When it comes together, there's nothing like it.

Running came later — first for fitness, then for the challenge of it. I ran my first half, got immediately addicted, and finished a full marathon in Rhode Island this spring. The same thing that gets you through mile 20 applies to most other hard things.

Solar is where I want to build.

I'm interested in solar energy — not because it's the obvious "future of energy" answer, but because the actual engineering is still being worked out. Efficiency gaps, manufacturing at scale, grid integration. There's real technical work left to do here.

I took Solar Engineering at BC and came away more convinced than ever. The intersection of materials science, systems thinking, and global-scale impact is exactly the kind of problem I want to spend my career on. Eventually, I want to build something in this space. Not just as an engineer inside a company — as a founder.

The throughline from taking apart a TV remote at age four to wanting to start a solar company isn't a straight line. But it's the same instinct: find something worth understanding, and then try to make it better.
